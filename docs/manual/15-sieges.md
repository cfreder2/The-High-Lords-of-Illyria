# Sieges {#sieges}

::: lore
*"Gate's open!"*

*"It's broken!"*

*"We're in!"*

— the 1st Oakhaven Bowmen, at Kestrel Point
:::

Every battle for a walled town or a capital is a siege: the defenders are inside the walls, and the attackers are outside, on the road before the gate. The walls are real. They stop men and arrows; the only way in on foot is the gate, once it is broken or standing open, or over the top on wings.

**Villages have no walls at all.** Fifty-one of the eighty places of Illyria are open villages: houses round a green and a tower house, with no wall, no gate and no towers. Nothing in this chapter applies to them: a fight for a village is a fight in the open among its houses, and it falls to whoever stands on it alone (chapter [](#banners)). The sieges are the eight capitals and the twenty-one walled towns, which stand at the fords and bridges and at the feet of the passes.

## The castle {#castle}

Every castle is different: square, long, many-sided or shield-shaped, with drum towers at the corners and on either side of the gate; capitals add an inner bailey round a great keep, with higher walls and a gate of its own. Inside are the keep, streets of houses, and a **courtyard** behind the gate, kept open for mustering, where a **flagpole** stands. Every tower and the keep fly their owner's banner, and the houses' roofs and the towers' conical roofs are in his colour too.

- **The wall-walk.** The walls are thick: 4 m (a capital's 4.5 m), so the walk along the top is wide enough for two men to pass, and men on it keep to the right. **Ramps** of earth faced with stone, 3 m wide, run up the inner faces from the courtyard to a landing beside the walk, three in a town (a capital's inner bailey has one more). Along the walk's outer edge stands a **parapet**: a low breastwork about knee high the whole way, and **merlons** higher than a man between **embrasures** a metre wide, every four metres. Nobody goes inside the **towers**: a stone **gallery** runs round the inner side of each one at the height of the walk, so men can walk right round the walls on top.
- **One archer to an embrasure.** A soldier on the walk can shoot out over the wall only from an embrasure, leaning into it; behind a merlon he cannot. *(A town's ring of about 290 m of wall has about 54 embrasures; a capital's outer ring of about 410 m, about 72.)* The garrison's archers are placed one to an embrasure, spread round the walls; the rest wait at the foot of a ramp, and are sent up to any embrasure left empty, by the dead or never manned.
- **How exposed the defenders are.** A defender at an embrasure stands against the breastwork, so about three quarters of him shows to a flat shot from far out; shots from close under the wall rise steeply and meet the parapet before him. Archers shooting up at the walk aim at the chest and shoot direct, only when the flight clears the parapet. The defenders have the height: a metre more reach for every metre above the target, and plunging shots that hit harder. They shoot first.
- **Falling.** A man thrown against the parapet stops there; one thrown above it goes over and falls to the foot of the wall. The parapet is no shelter from a catapult's stone coming down from above.

## Gates {#gates}

Every gate is two leaves of oak banded with iron, shut. While it stands it blocks walkers and missiles like the wall. It has **250 hit points for every metre** of its width: the gate in an outer wall, a town's or a capital's, is eight metres wide and has 2,000; the six-metre gate of a capital's inner bailey has 1,500.

- **Blows.** Attackers whose orders lead through a gate, or who stand idle at it with no enemy on their side, hack at it. A blow does a quarter of its damage to the timbers (at least 1): a squad of foot needs well over a minute.
- **Blasts do far more** than they do to a man, and most when they burst against the doors, falling off to nothing at the edge of the blast:

| What strikes the gate | To the gate | At most, a hit |
|---|---|---|
| A blow of sword, axe, club or claw | a quarter of the blow | 2 to 4 from a foot soldier, 11 from a giant |
| A Molotov, or a wizard's fireball | one and a half times its blast | 60 |
| A dragon's fireball | one and a half times its blast | 75 |
| A satchel charge, laid at the doors and set off with fire | three times its blast | 240 |
| A catapult's stone | five times its blast | 500 |

Four stones square on the doors, or nine satchels laid against them, break a town gate; stones that land a little wide do less, so count on more. Arrows do nothing to a gate.
- **Broken is broken.** The leaves hang shattered from their hinges and the timbers lie in the passage; everyone on the march finds the new way in, and missiles fly through. Nobody mends a gate: in the campaign a broken gate lies open for the rest of the war, whoever holds the town, and a damaged one keeps its wounds from siege to siege. Arrows stuck in the doors fall when they break. A town's own side never hacks at its own gate.

- **Gates swing.** The leaves take three seconds to open or shut, and block while they swing. Your own gate opens by itself for your men whose orders lead through it (they wait at it while it swings), stays open while any of them is near, and shuts behind them when the passage is clear. An enemy never opens it; he hacks at it, or walks through it when it stands open. Tap a gate of yours (men selected or not) and it opens, or shuts if it stood open, and is **held** so: a gate held shut stops your own men too, a gate held open lets everyone in. Its card says how it stands, with **Open gate** / **Close gate** and **Let go** (the gate goes back to opening for your men and shutting behind them); the keys are [[O]] and [[Shift]] + [[O]]. You can see how a gate stands only for your own.
- **A garrison keeps its gate.** When a foe comes near, the men inside take their places: the archers go up the ramps to the embrasures nearest the gate; the foot form a horseshoe behind the doors, facing the opening, so that whatever comes through is hit in front and on both sides. The garrison takes its stations when a foe comes within 70 m of the gate. The doors stay shut to what is outside, and when the foot are ready and the doors are failing, or there are few foes at them, are swung open for four seconds to draw some in. Break the gate, or take the walls first. Every garrison keeps its gate so, the neutral towns' and the computer lords' alike; your own gates are yours to open and shut.

**Walls do not break.** Stones and fireballs kill the men on them, and break gates; the stone stands.

## Engines {#engines}

The **catapult** is a wheeled mangonel: slow (0.8 m/s, and it wants roads), no use hand to hand, reloading for 14 seconds. Its arm always slams the same padded crossbar, so the stone leaves at a fixed 53 degrees and the crew wind the skein for the range: out to **150 m**, never nearer than **30 m** (it would come down on them). A stone smashes everything within 3 m, 100 damage at the centre falling off to the edge, throws men off their feet and off the wall-walks, and does five times its damage to a gate. Idle catapults throw at any foe within 140 m.

Tap a catapult, then a gate, and it batters the gate until it falls, then stands down. Ordered at a wall, it bombards the wall-walk there. In a campaign siege, the catapults in your stack come with you; in the dice they break the walls' bonus and fight two points stronger (chapter [](#siege-map)).

## Taking the courtyard {#capture-point}

This is the rule of the set battle (**Skirmish**, **The siege**: chapter [](#skirmish-siege)). There a siege is won by taking the heart of the town, not by killing every last man. Every moment, the flagpole in the courtyard counts the men of each side standing within **14 m** of it (alive, on the ground, not broken).

- If the **attackers** have more there, their flag climbs: **2 points, and one more for each man they are ahead by (at most 8 more)**, of 1,000, thirty times a second. One man more takes 11 seconds to raise it from the foot of the pole; eight or more, a little over 3 seconds.
- If the **defenders** have more, it comes down as fast. Even numbers hold it where it is. An empty courtyard lets it sink slowly (a point at a time: half a minute from the top).
- When the attackers' flag reaches the top, **the town is taken** for good and the battle ends in their victory, whoever is still standing elsewhere. (The battle is also won if every defender is dead, and lost if every attacker is.)

A bar at the top of the screen says how it goes: *Take the courtyard: your flag 45% · 12 of yours there, 3 of theirs (rising)*. On the pole, the defenders' flag comes down as yours goes up.

On the campaign map a siege is fought at the town's own walls and gate, and there is no separate battle to open; but the town changes hands by the **banner**, a different rule (chapter [](#banners)), and the two should not be confused:

| | The set battle's courtyard flag | The campaign's banner |
|---|---|---|
| Where | within 14 m of the flagpole | anywhere **inside the outer wall** (a village: on it, or within 10 m of it). Men at the foot of the wall or before the gate are not in the town |
| What is counted | men | armies with at least one man inside |
| Goes up when | the attackers have more men there than the defenders | your armies are inside and **nobody else's** are: one army of the town's own, or of a third lord, inside the wall stops it |
| How fast | 3 to 11 seconds, by how many more | about a minute for one army, about 20 seconds for eight |
| Comes down when | the defenders have more there | others have **more** armies inside than you; and it is lost at once if all of yours leave or fall |

So in the campaign it is not enough to hold the courtyard: the walls must be cleared, or the defenders drawn out or broken to the last army, before your banner will rise.

::: tip "Taking a town"
Batter the gate with stones, and with satchels laid at it and set off with fire, while your archers keep heads down on the walk near it; send fliers over the wall at the courtyard; then, in the set battle, put more men round the flagpole than they have: six men to their two take the courtyard in under six seconds. In the campaign, clear the town: your banner rises only when no army but yours has a man inside the wall.
:::

## Defending {#defending}

On the campaign map you see the walls from either side: the computer lords' attacks on your towns are fought at your gate, by your garrison, with your archers on the walk, as any siege is. Your gate is yours: your own men never hack at it, and everyone else does. If an enemy is raising its banner in a town of yours, attacking it there means storming your own walls: they hold them now. The battle captain is ready for the other way round: besieging a town of yours, the enemy turns his catapults on your gate. Keep the archers in the embrasures and their reserve at the foot of the ramps, and a solid block behind the gate. While one army of yours has a man alive inside the wall, the enemy's banner cannot go up; once it is up, an army of yours inside stops it, and more armies than he has brings it down.
